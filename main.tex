% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Hierarchical multi-label classifiers often enforce the class hierarchy
with the max-constraint of C-HMCNN (Giunchiglia \& Lukasiewicz, NeurIPS
2020)~\cite{chmcnn}, which acts in two places: in the training loss and
in the predictions. We ask when this constraint actually helps, and
through which of the two. To answer, we built \textsc{hmc-torch}, an
open-source platform that runs text, genomic, and image benchmarks
through one pipeline and one evaluation protocol---model and threshold
selection on validation data, three seeds, test data used once---and
lets the constraint be switched on separately at training and at
inference. On 27 datasets, reconciling a model's predictions at
inference improves AUPRC on 25 of them, by a small margin (median
$+0.002$) that tracks how much the base model violates the hierarchy,
not the shape of the hierarchy itself. Adding
the constraint to training, either as a hinge penalty or as C-HMCNN's
MCLoss, gives no consistent further gain over plain cross-entropy
followed by reconciliation. A plain MLP with reconciliation comes close to the
published neural baselines on FunCat, Gene Ontology, and four
cross-domain datasets, while ontology-based methods stay clearly
ahead; on WOS, a fine-tuned SPECTER2 reaches $0.863 \pm 0.006$
Micro-F1, just below the best published results. A sparse,
differentiable implementation of the constraint makes all of this run
at ordinary batch sizes on 4{,}000-class Gene Ontology hierarchies.
We also show that evaluation choices---threshold selection on the test
split, fixed training budgets---moved our own results by more than the
gaps between published methods.

\noindent\textbf{Availability:} \url{https://github.com/sette/hmc-torch} \textbar{}
\url{https://pypi.org/project/hmc-torch/}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

In Hierarchical Multi-Label Classification (HMC), each instance can
belong to several classes at once, and those classes are organized in a
predefined hierarchy---a tree or a DAG. The task comes up all the time
in science: sorting papers into topic taxonomies (ArXiv, WOS),
annotating proteins with Gene Ontology (GO) terms, or placing diatoms
in a morphological hierarchy.

Methods for HMC have grown steadily more elaborate, from local
classifiers trained one level at a time~\cite{silla2011hmc} to global
models with graph neural networks~\cite{zhou2020hiagm} and learned
ontology representations~\cite{www2022ontology}. One idea from this
line of work is both simple and effective: C-HMCNN, by Giunchiglia \&
Lukasiewicz~\cite{chmcnn}. On top of any multi-label network $h$, a
max constraint module (MCM) sets each class's score to the maximum
over its subclasses, so a parent is never predicted with lower
probability than any of its children, and a dedicated loss (MCLoss)
teaches the network when to rely on those subclasses. On a GPU, the
module is computed with an ancestor mask over all class pairs, which
their public code calls \texttt{R}; we refer to it as the
\emph{R-matrix}.

C-HMCNN already works with any base network $h$, and it performs well
on the standard benchmarks. What is less clear is \emph{why} and
\emph{when}. The constraint acts in two places---it changes the
training signal (through MCLoss) and it changes the predictions
(through MCM)---and published ablations measure them only in the
combinations their authors used. It is also rarely compared across
modalities under one protocol: HMC results on text, genomics, and
images come from different codebases, splits, and threshold rules,
which makes it hard to tell a better method from a friendlier
evaluation. This paper asks a simple question: \emph{when does the
hierarchical constraint actually help, and through which of its two
mechanisms?}

To answer it we built \textsc{hmc-torch}, a platform in which the
constraint is a building block that can be switched on independently
at training time (as a consistency loss or as MCLoss) and at inference
time (as bottom-up reconciliation), for text encoders and tabular
features---including pre-extracted image features---alike. Three design decisions shape the system:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Keep data and models apart.} Data contracts
    (\texttt{DatasetBundle}, \texttt{Split}) sit between dataset
    loading and model training, and every feature encoder follows the
    same \texttt{fit/transform} protocol regardless of modality.
  \item \textbf{Make the hierarchy type explicit.} Trees (FunCat) and
    DAGs (Gene Ontology) are distinct types, each with its own
    reconciliation, so single-parent and multi-parent semantics are
    never silently mixed up.
  \item \textbf{Treat the R-matrix as infrastructure.} The
    ancestor-closure matrix is a differentiable layer that any head can
    use, which is what makes it possible to switch it on and off and
    measure what it actually contributes.
\end{enumerate}

We use three related terms throughout (defined precisely in
Section~\ref{sec:terms}): the \emph{dense R-matrix} is the explicit
$C \times C$ ancestor-closure matrix; the \emph{R-matrix constraint} is
the consistency property itself; and the \emph{sparse R-matrix
formulation} is our graph-based version, which enforces the same
property without ever building the dense matrix.

In short, this paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{A modular HMC platform} covering 27 datasets---text,
    genomic tabular data, Gene Ontology, microscopy, email, and medical
    imaging---behind a shared encoder/head interface, released on PyPI.
  \item \textbf{A single evaluation protocol} applied to all of them:
    model selection and decision thresholds on the validation split,
    the test split touched once, three seeds, and one script that
    regenerates every number in the paper.
  \item \textbf{A decomposition of the constraint} into its
    training-time and inference-time parts (three training objectives
    $\times$ two inference modes), which shows where each part helps.
  \item \textbf{An explanation of the size of the gain}: it does not
    follow the share of internal nodes in the hierarchy, but it does
    follow how much the base model's scores violate the hierarchy,
    which can be measured before deciding to use the constraint.
  \item \textbf{A sparse, differentiable formulation} of the constraint
    that runs in $O(B \cdot E)$ memory, which makes MCLoss trainable
    at ordinary batch sizes on Gene Ontology hierarchies.
  \item \textbf{A like-for-like comparison with published results},
    stating explicitly where our protocol differs from theirs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{HMC Methods}

The first HMC approaches handled each level of the hierarchy
separately, with local classifiers~\cite{silla2011hmc,cerri2014hmc}.
Global methods, by contrast, predict all classes together, and HMCN
(Wehrmann et al.~\cite{weihs2018hmc}) sits in between: it combines a
local output per level with a global output and is trained with both a
local and a global loss. In text classification,
HiAGM~\cite{zhou2020hiagm} encodes the label hierarchy with a
structure encoder (a Graph Convolutional Network or a TreeLSTM) and
fuses it with the text representation. The hierarchy shapes the
representation and, through a recursive regularization, keeps the
classifier weights of parents and children close, but every node is
still predicted independently with a fixed threshold of 0.5, so
HiAGM's predictions come with no coherence guarantee.
HTC-infoMAX~\cite{htcinfomax} builds on the HiAGM-LA variant and adds
text--label mutual information maximization and a label prior matching
loss. On protein function prediction
(the FunCat and GO benchmarks), Surj~\cite{www2022ontology} set the
state of the art in AUPRC by learning class embeddings directly from
the structure of the Gene Ontology.

For tabular and genomic data, CLUS-HMC~\cite{clus} builds decision
trees that respect the hierarchy, HMCN~\cite{weihs2018hmc} shares
information between levels through its local and global outputs, and
C-HMCNN~\cite{chmcnn} introduced the max constraint module and loss
that this work builds on.

\subsection{The R-Matrix Constraint}

The hierarchy constraint itself---a sample that belongs to a class
also belongs to all of its ancestors---is part of the definition of
HMC, and many earlier neural models only satisfy it after a
post-processing step~\cite{cerri2014hmc,weihs2018hmc}. C-HMCNN~\cite{chmcnn}
was designed to make predictions coherent by construction. Its max
constraint module (MCM) is computed with an $N \times N$ binary mask
with $R_{ij} = 1$ exactly when class $j$ is a subclass of class $i$
(that is, $i$ is an ancestor of $j$, or $i=j$), which guarantees
$\forall (i,j): \text{ancestor}(i,j) \implies p_i \geq p_j$ for any
threshold. Its max constraint loss (MCLoss) is a modified binary
cross-entropy in which, for a positive class, only subclasses that are
also positive enter the maximum, so the network learns when to
delegate a prediction to a subclass. Both pieces sit on top of an
arbitrary base network $h$.

To be clear, neither the mask nor the idea of building it into the
model is ours; both come from Giunchiglia \& Lukasiewicz (2020). What
we add is (i) a platform in which the same component is shared by
the text and tabular pipelines, (ii) a separation between its
training-time and inference-time uses, so their effects can be
measured independently, and (iii) a sparse formulation that avoids the
dense mask on large hierarchies.

\subsection{Modular ML Platforms}

Libraries such as HuggingFace Transformers and PyTorch Lightning have
shown how much a modular, composable design helps in practice. We are
not aware of a comparable library for HMC. \texttt{hmc-torch} tries to fill that gap
with abstractions built for hierarchical problems, and we try to be
explicit about which parts are new (the platform design and the
systematic evaluation) and which we inherit (the R-matrix constraint
and the transformer encoders).

% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{The HMC-Torch Methodology}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The platform rests on three ideas: (1) typed data contracts that keep
concerns cleanly separated; (2) hierarchy-aware modeling, where the
class taxonomy shapes the architecture instead of being patched in
afterwards; and (3) a single encoder protocol, so the same pipeline
can handle text, tabular, image, and sequence data (the image and
sequence encoders are still stubs).

\subsection{Data Contracts}

The central abstraction is the \texttt{DatasetBundle}, which holds
everything a model needs to know about a dataset:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{\texttt{Split}}. One partition of the data (train,
    valid, or test). It contains a feature matrix
    $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$, a binary label matrix
    $Y \in \{0,1\}^{N \times C}$ (where $C$ is the number of class
    nodes), per-level label vectors
    $Y_{\text{local}} = [\mathbf{y}^{(0)}, \ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(L-1)}]$
    for the $L$ levels of the hierarchy, and, optionally, sample
    identifiers used to check for duplicates.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{Hierarchy}}. The class taxonomy, represented
    either as a \texttt{TreeHierarchy} (one parent per node, as in
    FunCat) or a \texttt{DagHierarchy} (several parents allowed, as in
    the Gene Ontology). Both offer the same interface: \texttt{nodes},
    \texttt{levels}, \texttt{node\_index}, \texttt{parents(child)},
    \texttt{children(parent)}, \texttt{ancestors(node)}, and the
    \texttt{r\_matrix} (ancestor closure). Because the type is explicit,
    tree-only logic cannot accidentally be applied to a DAG---a mistake
    that is otherwise easy to make.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{FeatureMetadata}}. Describes the features: their
    modality (tabular, text, vision, protein, expression),
    dimensionality, missing values, sparsity, and whether raw data is
    available for end-to-end training.
\end{enumerate}

These contracts draw a clear line between loading data and training
models. Dataset adapters (ARFF parsers, JSONL readers) and models meet
at the same \texttt{Split} layout, so adding a dataset or a head does
not require touching the rest of the pipeline. This is what lets a
single experiment script run all 27 datasets in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, instead of one ad-hoc loader per benchmark.

\subsection{Feature Encoders}

Every feature encoder implements the same two methods:

\begin{center}
\texttt{encoder.fit(train\_split)} $\rightarrow$
\texttt{encoder.transform(any\_split)} $\rightarrow$
\texttt{Split}
\end{center}

\texttt{fit} learns whatever the encoder needs from the split it
receives (scaler statistics, imputation values, feature-selection
masks), and \texttt{transform} applies it, returning a new
\texttt{Split} with the encoded features. In our experiments,
\texttt{fit} sees only the training partition, so neither the
validation nor the test split influences the fitted statistics. The
fitted encoder is an ordinary object and can be reused on any split.

We implement the protocol for the modalities used in this paper, plus
two stubs for future work:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Tabular} (\texttt{TabularFeatureEncoder}). Mean
    imputation and standardization, both on by default, with optional
    feature selection by variance threshold or by mutual information
    with the labels. The experiments in this paper use only imputation
    and standardization.
  \item \textbf{Text} (\texttt{TextFeatureEncoder}). Wraps a
    HuggingFace transformer (SPECTER2-base by default) and uses the CLS
    token as the document representation. When no raw text is
    available, pre-extracted features pass through unchanged. The
    encoder itself is always frozen; end-to-end fine-tuning lives in a
    separate E2E model that owns the transformer.
  \item \textbf{Protein} and \textbf{Vision}
    (\texttt{ProteinFeatureEncoder}, \texttt{VisionFeatureEncoder}).
    For now these are experimental stubs that pass pre-extracted
    features through unchanged. The plan is to back them with ESM-2
    sequence embeddings and DINOv2/ConvNeXt image backbones.
\end{itemize}

Since encoders and heads agree on the \texttt{Split} contract, we can
swap the tabular and text paths without changing anything downstream.

\subsection{Hierarchical Classification Heads}

The platform ships three heads:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{GlobalSigmoidHead}. A single multi-layer perceptron
    that scores all $C$ class nodes at once:
    $\text{MLP}(\mathbf{x}) \rightarrow \sigma(\cdot) \rightarrow
    [0,1]^C$. Every experiment in this paper uses this head.
  \item \textbf{LocalLevelHead}. One MLP per level of the hierarchy,
    each predicting only the classes at its depth:
    $\text{MLP}_\ell(\mathbf{x}) \rightarrow [0,1]^{C_\ell}$. The MLPs
    are trained together, with a BCE loss per level, and
    \texttt{to\_global} maps their outputs back into a single score
    vector using the hierarchy's node index.
  \item \textbf{TreePathHead}. Per-level heads for strict trees, decoded
    by taking the argmax at each level. The idea is to force each sample
    onto a single root-to-leaf path by restricting every level to the
    children of the node picked above it. That masking is not
    implemented yet, so the head is experimental and plays no part in
    our experiments.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{R-Matrix: The Hierarchical Constraint Layer}

The R-matrix~\cite{chmcnn} is at the heart of how we model the
hierarchy. Starting from the adjacency matrix $A$ of the class
hierarchy (with $A_{ij}=1$ if $i$ is a direct child of $j$), we take
its transitive closure:

\begin{equation}
R_{ij} = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } i = j \text{ or } \exists \text{ path } j \rightarrow \ldots \rightarrow i \text{ in } A \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:rmatrix}
\end{equation}

Each edge of $A$ points from a child to its parent, so a path
$j \rightarrow \ldots \rightarrow i$ climbs one level per hop and ends
at an ancestor of $j$. Take the ArXiv label \texttt{cs.AI}: it produces
the edges \texttt{cs.AI}$\rightarrow$\texttt{cs} and
\texttt{cs}$\rightarrow$root, so there is a two-hop path
\texttt{cs.AI}$\rightarrow$\texttt{cs}$\rightarrow$root and
$R_{\text{root},\text{cs.AI}}=1$, even though root is not a direct
parent of \texttt{cs.AI}. In other words, the closure turns the direct
edges into \emph{every} ancestor--descendant pair. $R \in \{0,1\}^{C
\times C}$ marks exactly those pairs, plus the diagonal $i=j$, which
has to be added separately because $A$ has no self-loops.

Again, the R-matrix itself is due to Giunchiglia \& Lukasiewicz. The
platform exposes it in two places: in the training objective and at
inference.

\textbf{At inference: reconciliation.} At prediction time the
constraint is enforced exactly by propagating maxima from the bottom
up:

\begin{equation}
p_i^{\text{rec}} = \max\left(p_i,\; \max_{j \in \text{children}(i)} p_j^{\text{rec}}\right)
\label{eq:reconcile}
\end{equation}

Going from the deepest level to the shallowest guarantees that
$\forall (i,j): \text{ancestor}(i,j) \implies p_i^{\text{rec}} \geq
p_j^{\text{rec}}$, with no violations left after inference. This is
exactly the output of C-HMCNN's max constraint module (MCM)~\cite{chmcnn}.
Note that only \emph{internal} nodes can change: a leaf has no
descendants, so its score is left as is.

\textbf{During training: three objectives.} We compare three ways of
training the same network. The first is plain binary cross-entropy
(BCE), which ignores the hierarchy. The second adds a hinge penalty on
every ancestor--descendant pair where the descendant scores higher:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{hinge}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} + \frac{\lambda}{B \cdot |P|}
\sum_{b=1}^{B} \sum_{(i,j) \in P}
\max\left(0,\; p_{b,j} - p_{b,i}\right)
\label{eq:hierloss}
\end{equation}

where $P = \{(i,j) : R_{ij}=1,\ i \neq j\}$ is the set of
ancestor--descendant pairs and $\lambda = 1$. It is similar in spirit to the violation penalty
of HMCN~\cite{weihs2018hmc}, which is squared and limited to direct
parent--child pairs. The third is C-HMCNN's MCLoss~\cite{chmcnn}, a
cross-entropy computed on constrained outputs:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}} = \mathrm{BCE}\left((1-\mathbf{y}) \odot \mathrm{MCM}(\mathbf{p})
+ \mathbf{y} \odot \mathrm{MCM}(\mathbf{y} \odot \mathbf{p}),\; \mathbf{y}\right)
\label{eq:mcloss}
\end{equation}

where MCM is the reconciliation of Eq.~\ref{eq:reconcile}. For a
negative label, the loss sees the constrained score, so a parent is
penalized when any of its children fires; for a positive label, only
children that are also positive can lift the parent's score, which
teaches the network when to delegate a prediction to a subclass.

\label{sec:method-grid}Crossing the three objectives with the two inference modes (raw and
reconciled) gives six configurations of one and the same network. This
is how the platform turns the question ``does the constraint help?''
into two separate ones: does it help as a training signal, and does it
help as an inference-time correction? C-HMCNN always uses MCLoss
together with MCM, so its own ablation~\cite{chmcnn} covers only part
of this grid.

For large label spaces, the sparse formulation works on the hierarchy
graph shown in Figure~\ref{fig:sparse_graph}: the label structure stays
explicit rather than being folded into a dense matrix.

\subsection{Sparse R-Matrix: Scaling to Large DAGs}
\label{sec:sparse}

The dense R-matrix needs $O(C^2)$ memory, where $C$ is the number of
class nodes (below, $E$ is the number of parent--child edges). For Gene
Ontology datasets with more than 4{,}000 classes, storing the matrix
takes tens of megabytes, which is manageable; the real cost comes from
applying it, because the batched max creates a
batch$\times C\times C$ tensor. C-HMCNN~\cite{chmcnn} runs the dense
version on these datasets with a batch size of 4; at larger batch
sizes the memory quickly runs out.

The sparse formulation never builds the matrix. It keeps the list of
parent--child edges and repeats one vectorized step: every parent
takes the maximum of its current score and the scores of its direct
children (a \texttt{scatter\_reduce} over the edge list). After as
many steps as the hierarchy is deep, every ancestor is at least as
high as all of its descendants---the same scores the dense version
produces, which we verified (maximum absolute difference 0) on all 27
hierarchies used in this paper. Memory grows with the size of the graph,
$O(B \cdot E)$ for a batch of $B$, instead of $O(B \cdot C^2)$. The
operation is differentiable, so MCLoss (Eq.~\ref{eq:mcloss}) can be
trained through it: on cellcycle\_GO ($C{=}4{,}126$, $E{=}5{,}827$),
the dense batched step at batch size 64 needs about 8.7\,GB in float64,
while the sparse one needs a few megabytes.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Every number in Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:discussion}
comes from two scripts in the \texttt{experiments/} folder of the
repository: \texttt{run\_protocol.py} runs all datasets, all six
configurations, and three seeds, plus the GBDT baseline, and
\texttt{run\_wos\_e2e.py} runs the end-to-end fine-tuning on WOS. A
third script, \texttt{make\_paper\_artifacts.py}, turns their output
into the tables, figures, and statistical tests of this paper. Each run writes a JSON
record with the dataset sizes, hyperparameters, selected epoch and
threshold, and every metric, for each seed and configuration. The
command-line entry point (\texttt{python -m hmc.main}) follows the same
evaluation protocol: the decision threshold, when tuned, is chosen on
the validation split.

\subsection{Terminology}
\label{sec:terms}

To avoid ambiguity, we stick to the following terms:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Path.} A chain of edges in the adjacency matrix $A$
    that goes from a node up to one of its ancestors, one level per hop
    (edges in $A$ point from child to parent). \emph{Ancestor} and
    \emph{descendant} always refer to this relation. Paths are only a
    means to an end: the closure turns the $E$ direct edges into the
    full set of ancestor--descendant pairs stored in $R$.
  \item \textbf{Dense R-matrix.} The explicit $C \times C$
    ancestor-closure matrix. It is exact, but its memory cost is
    quadratic.
  \item \textbf{R-matrix constraint.} The consistency property itself:
    an ancestor must score at least as high as each of its descendants.
  \item \textbf{Sparse R-matrix formulation.} Our graph-based version of
    the same constraint, expressed directly on the hierarchy's edges
    without building the dense matrix.
\end{itemize}

By the \textbf{depth} of a node we mean its shortest distance from the
root; it is what the per-level analysis of Section~\ref{sec:results}
groups labels by.

\subsection{Other Components}

\textbf{Losses.} Besides the three objectives above, the platform
implements prevalence-weighted BCE, focal loss, and a
document--document contrastive loss (available only in the GNN
trainer); none of them is used in this paper, and a document--label
contrastive variant is still experimental.

\textbf{Calibration.} Per-node Platt scaling and isotonic regression,
both fitted on the validation set. We evaluate Platt scaling on the
GBDT baseline in Section~\ref{sec:calib}; isotonic regression is
implemented but not used here.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Every dataset is evaluated the same way:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item Features are standardized with training-split statistics
    (mean imputation first for the tabular datasets).
  \item Models are trained on the training split. After each epoch the
    micro-AUPRC of the reconciled outputs is measured on the validation
    split, and training stops after 20 epochs without improvement (10
    for text), keeping the best checkpoint, with a cap of 200 epochs
    (100 for text).
  \item The decision threshold is the value in $\{0.05, 0.10, \ldots,
    0.95\}$ that maximizes Micro-F1 on the \emph{validation} split, and
    is then applied unchanged to the test split. The test split is used
    once, for the final metrics.
  \item On the ARFF benchmarks (FunCat, GO, and the cross-domain
    datasets) the model is then retrained on training plus validation
    data for the selected number of epochs, the convention of the
    published baselines~\cite{weihs2018hmc,chmcnn}; the threshold still
    comes from the first, train-only model. On text we do not retrain,
    following HPT~\cite{hpt}.
  \item Every configuration is run with three seeds (0, 42, 123); we
    report the mean and standard deviation.
\end{itemize}

We report micro-averaged F1 (at the validation-chosen threshold) and
micro-averaged AUPRC, which does not depend on the threshold and is the
standard metric on FunCat and GO~\cite{weihs2018hmc,chmcnn}. Both are
computed over the evaluated labels (every node except the root).

\subsection{Models}

\textbf{MLP.} All six configurations of Section~\ref{sec:method-grid}
share one network: a three-layer MLP (GlobalSigmoidHead) with ReLU,
dropout 0.3, and a sigmoid output per class, trained with AdamW
(learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$). On the ARFF
benchmarks the hidden size is the per-dataset value used by
C-HMCNN~\cite{chmcnn} (except expr\_FUN, where the platform's registry
uses 1{,}250 instead of 1{,}000; church and pheno, which C-HMCNN does
not cover, use 300 and 500); on text it is 512. The batch size is 32 on FunCat and the
cross-domain datasets and 64 on GO and text. Text inputs are frozen
SPECTER2-base~\cite{specter2} embeddings (768 dimensions, CLS token).
We do not tune hyperparameters per dataset.

\textbf{End-to-end fine-tuning (WOS).} SPECTER2-base with the same MLP
head on top, fine-tuned for up to four epochs (learning rate
$2\times10^{-5}$ for the transformer and $10^{-4}$ for the head, batch
size 8, inputs truncated to 512 tokens), with the epoch and the
threshold chosen on the validation split. It is trained on the 30{,}070
training documents only, the setup of the published WOS
baselines~\cite{hpt}.

\textbf{GBDT (FunCat).} One-vs-rest HistGradientBoosting from
scikit-learn (100 iterations, no early stopping), one classifier per
node with at least five positive training examples; the other nodes
get their training prevalence. Its outputs are reconciled like the
MLP's.

\textbf{Published baselines.} HMCN-F~\cite{weihs2018hmc}, the
feed-forward variant of HMCN; C-HMCNN~\cite{chmcnn}; and
Surj~\cite{www2022ontology}, which learns class representations from
the ontology, on the ARFF benchmarks. On WOS, the numbers reported by
HPT~\cite{hpt}: HPT, HGCLR~\cite{hgclr}, and BERT-based
reimplementations of HiAGM~\cite{zhou2020hiagm},
HTCInfoMax~\cite{htcinfomax}, and HiMatch~\cite{himatch}. All published
numbers are taken from the papers, not rerun.

Three observations hold across families.

\textbf{Reconciliation alone gives a small, consistent gain.}
Reconciling the outputs of a BCE-trained model raises test AUPRC on
25 of the 27 datasets (Wilcoxon signed-rank test over datasets,
$p=2\times10^{-7}$), by a median of 0.002. Micro-F1 goes up on 24 of
them ($p=10^{-5}$, median $+0.001$). The gain is free---no retraining, no
extra parameters---and it never costs much where it does not help.

\textbf{The hinge penalty adds nothing measurable.} Training with
Eq.~\ref{eq:hierloss} gives the same results as plain BCE, with or
without reconciliation, on every family. The network already produces
few large violations; penalizing them during training changes little
that reconciliation would not fix afterwards anyway.

\textbf{MCLoss only works together with reconciliation.} Evaluated on
its raw outputs, a model trained with MCLoss is much worse than the
BCE baseline (for instance 0.649 vs.\ 0.956 AUPRC on ImCLEF07A).
This is by design: MCLoss computes the loss on constrained outputs, so
the network learns to leave parent scores low and let the maximum over
children lift them. With reconciliation at inference, MCLoss recovers
and ends up level with BCE plus reconciliation: slightly ahead on
FunCat and EUR-Lex, slightly behind on GO and the cross-domain
datasets, and within 0.007 AUPRC on every dataset except Diatoms,
where it trails by 0.013 (Table~\ref{tab:decomp-full}). C-HMCNN's own
ablation~\cite{chmcnn} found MCLoss better than training through the
constraint module with plain BCE; our results add that it is not
better than the simpler option of training with BCE and reconciling
afterwards.

In short, the practical value of the hierarchical constraint in our
experiments comes from its inference-time half. Its training-time half
either does nothing (hinge) or is a different way of reaching the same
place (MCLoss).

\subsection{How Large Can the Gain Be?}
\label{sec:structure}

Reconciliation can only change internal nodes: a leaf has no
descendants, so its score is left as is. A natural guess is therefore
that the gain grows with the share of internal nodes, which varies a
lot across our datasets (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): 4 of 56 evaluated
labels on Enron, 7 of 61 on AAPD, about a third on FunCat, and about
half on GO. Micro-averaged metrics weigh every (sample, label) pair
equally, so the relevant share is that of \emph{positive} labels on
internal nodes, which is higher still---from about 40\% on ArXiv and
AAPD to 90\% on GO, since every positive leaf brings its ancestors
along.

Neither share predicts the gain (Figure~\ref{fig:structure}, left;
Spearman $\rho=0.08$ across the 27 datasets). GO, where 90\% of the
positive labels are internal, gains only a little more than ArXiv, where
40\% are (+0.003 vs.\ +0.001 AUPRC on average). What does predict it is how much the base model actually
violates the hierarchy. To measure this we retrained the BCE model of
every dataset (seed 0) and recorded, on the test split, how often and
by how much reconciliation raises a score. The average raise per
evaluated score correlates clearly with the gain in AUPRC
(Figure~\ref{fig:structure}, right; $\rho=0.54$, $p=0.004$). Diatoms
is the clearest case: only 8\% of its internal-node scores are raised,
but by 0.009 on average, and it has the largest gain (+0.011 AUPRC).
On GO the model raises about 40\% of the internal-node scores, but by
only 0.002 on average, and the gains stay small.

The practical reading is that the hierarchy's shape tells you whether
reconciliation \emph{can} change anything, while the model's own
violations, measurable on validation data without any retraining, tell
you whether it \emph{will}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{When Does the Constraint Help?}

Putting the results together, the hierarchical constraint helps in a
narrow but dependable way:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Use it at inference.} Reconciling a model's outputs is
    almost never harmful and usually a little helpful. It also
    guarantees consistent predictions, which matters on its own for
    any application that reads the labels as a hierarchy.
  \item \textbf{Do not expect much from it during training.} A hinge
    penalty did nothing measurable, and MCLoss reached the same place
    as BCE plus reconciliation by a different route.
  \item \textbf{Look at the model, not just the hierarchy.} A deep
    hierarchy with many internal nodes does not guarantee a gain; what
    matters is how much the model's scores violate it, which can be
    checked on validation data in seconds.
  \item \textbf{It is not a substitute for knowledge about the labels.}
    Surj's lead on FunCat and GO comes from learning class
    representations from the ontology---information a binary ancestor
    mask cannot carry.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Choices Matter as Much as Methods}

Several of our own numbers moved by more than the gaps between
published methods when we fixed the evaluation protocol, without any
change to the models. A preliminary WOS run that trained on training
plus validation data and chose the threshold on the test split
reported 0.874 Micro-F1, above HPT; under the published protocol the
same model gets $0.863 \pm 0.006$. Preliminary Gene Ontology runs with
a fixed budget of 20 epochs reached about 0.28 average AUPRC; with
early stopping on validation the same MLP reaches 0.41. On Diatoms, a
fixed 50 epochs at batch size 128 gave 0.45 AUPRC; early stopping at
batch size 32 gives 0.785. We think this is where a shared platform is
most useful: one protocol, applied the same way to every dataset and
every method, so that differences between methods are not confounded
with differences in evaluation.

\subsection{Limitations}

We study one architecture (an MLP head, on frozen or fine-tuned
encoders) and do not tune hyperparameters per dataset, which likely
explains part of the remaining gap to the tuned published baselines.
Two training budgets were binding: EUR-Lex reached the 100-epoch cap,
and on WOS two of the three fine-tuning seeds peaked at the last of
four epochs. End-to-end fine-tuning was run on WOS only. RCV1-V2 is missing for
licensing reasons, and our ArXiv and AAPD splits have no published
counterparts. The sparse formulation is exact by construction; its
contribution is practical (memory and batch size), not a new model.

\subsection{Attribution of Contributions}

We want to be explicit about what we build on:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{R-matrix constraint:} Giunchiglia \& Lukasiewicz (NeurIPS
    2020)~\cite{chmcnn}, namely the max constraint module, MCLoss, and
    the ancestor mask used to compute them. The hierarchical network we
    compare against is HMCN-F~\cite{weihs2018hmc}, the feed-forward
    variant of HMCN.
  \item \textbf{SPECTER2 embeddings:} Singh et al.~(EMNLP
    2023)~\cite{specter2}.
  \item \textbf{FunCat/GO benchmarks and splits:} the standard HMC
    literature~\cite{clus,cerri2014hmc}.
\end{itemize}

What is new in this work is the platform, the single protocol applied
to 27 datasets, the train-time/inference-time decomposition and the
analysis of what drives its gain, and the sparse, differentiable
implementation of the constraint.

% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

We presented \textsc{hmc-torch}, a modular platform for hierarchical
multi-label classification, and used it to ask when the hierarchical
constraint of C-HMCNN helps. Under one protocol on 27 datasets, the
answer is: mostly at inference time, by a small and consistent margin
that grows with how much the base model violates the hierarchy. Training-time
penalties added nothing beyond that, and a plain MLP with
reconciliation stayed close to the published neural baselines on
FunCat, GO, and the cross-domain benchmarks, while ontology-aware
methods remain clearly ahead. Integrating ontology embeddings and
finishing the protein sequence encoder (ESM-2), today a placeholder,
are the natural next steps.

and can be installed with \texttt{pip install hmc-torch}
(\url{https://pypi.org/project/hmc-torch/}).
