% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
We present \textsc{hmc-torch}, an open-source platform for Hierarchical
Multi-Label Classification (HMC) built around the R-matrix constraint
of Giunchiglia \& Lukasiewicz (NeurIPS 2020)~\cite{chmcnn}. We package
the constraint as a reusable component shared by encoders and heads
across modalities, and we separate its two uses---during training and
at inference---so each can be switched on and measured on its own. The
platform keeps data contracts (\texttt{DatasetBundle},
\texttt{Hierarchy}), feature encoders, and HMC heads separate, which
lets us run the same pipeline on 28 datasets with tree- and
DAG-structured hierarchies. On WOS we reach 0.8743 Micro-F1 (single
seed), on par with the best published result (0.8716), although our
protocol differs from theirs in ways that favor us. On ArXiv and AAPD,
which we re-split ourselves, frozen SPECTER2 embeddings with the
R-matrix reach 0.730 and 0.823 Micro-F1, and our FunCat and EUR-Lex 57K
baselines are competitive. We also describe a sparse formulation of the R-matrix
that enforces the same hierarchical consistency in $O(C{+}E)$ memory
instead of $O(C^2)$. On nine Gene Ontology datasets, where the dense
matrix would run out of GPU memory at our batch size of 64, sparse
reconciliation improves AUPRC on every dataset.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Availability:} \url{https://github.com/sette/hmc-torch} \textbar{}
\url{https://pypi.org/project/hmc-torch/}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

In Hierarchical Multi-Label Classification (HMC), each instance can
belong to several classes at once, and those classes are organized in a
predefined hierarchy---a tree or a DAG. The task comes up all the time
in science: sorting papers into topic taxonomies (ArXiv, WOS),
annotating proteins with Gene Ontology (GO) terms, or placing diatoms
in a morphological hierarchy.

Methods for HMC have grown steadily more elaborate, from local
classifiers trained one level at a time~\cite{silla2011hmc} to global
models with graph neural networks~\cite{zhou2020hiagm} and learned
ontology representations~\cite{www2022ontology}. One idea from this
line of work is both simple and effective: C-HMCNN, by Giunchiglia \&
Lukasiewicz~\cite{chmcnn}. On top of any multi-label network $h$, a
max constraint module (MCM) sets each class's score to the maximum
over its subclasses, so a parent is never predicted with lower
probability than any of its children, and a dedicated loss (MCLoss)
teaches the network when to rely on those subclasses. On a GPU, the
module is computed with an ancestor mask over all class pairs, which
their public code calls \texttt{R}; we refer to it as the
\emph{R-matrix}.

C-HMCNN already works with any base network $h$. What remains hard is
using it outside its original setting---tabular benchmarks, a fixed
MLP, a dense $C \times C$ mask---and telling apart how much of its
benefit comes from training and how much from inference.
\textsc{hmc-torch} addresses both. The R-matrix becomes a building
block of the platform, usable during training (as a consistency loss)
and at inference (as bottom-up reconciliation), with text, tabular,
and image encoders alike. Three design decisions shape the rest of the
system:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Keep data and models apart.} Data contracts
    (\texttt{DatasetBundle}, \texttt{Split}) sit between dataset
    loading and model training, and every feature encoder follows the
    same \texttt{fit/transform} protocol regardless of modality.
  \item \textbf{Make the hierarchy type explicit.} Trees (FunCat) and
    DAGs (Gene Ontology) are distinct types, each with its own
    reconciliation, so single-parent and multi-parent semantics are
    never silently mixed up.
  \item \textbf{Treat the R-matrix as infrastructure.} The
    ancestor-closure matrix is a differentiable layer that any head can
    use, which is what makes it possible to switch it on and off and
    measure what it actually contributes.
\end{enumerate}

We use three related terms throughout (defined precisely in
Section~\ref{sec:terms}): the \emph{dense R-matrix} is the explicit
$C \times C$ ancestor-closure matrix; the \emph{R-matrix constraint} is
the consistency property itself; and the \emph{sparse R-matrix
formulation} is our graph-based version, which enforces the same
property without ever building the dense matrix.

In short, this paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item A modular HMC platform that covers 28 datasets---text, genomic
    tabular data, Gene Ontology, microscopy, email, and medical
    imaging---behind a shared encoder/head interface, with each run
    saving everything needed to inspect it later.
  \item Text results on par with the best published numbers on WOS,
    plus strong results on our own ArXiv and AAPD splits, using frozen
    or fine-tuned SPECTER2 with the R-matrix constraint.
  \item A sparse, graph-based formulation of the R-matrix that runs in
    $O(C{+}E)$ memory and enforces the same constraint on large DAGs.
  \item Competitive FunCat results with simple models, together with a
    frank look at where the R-matrix falls short of ontology-based
    methods.
  \item An open-source release with GPU support and reproducible
    experiment artifacts.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{HMC Methods}

The first HMC approaches handled each level of the hierarchy
separately, with local classifiers~\cite{silla2011hmc,cerri2014hmc}.
Global methods, by contrast, predict all classes together, and HMCN
(Wehrmann et al.~\cite{weihs2018hmc}) sits in between: it combines a
local output per level with a global output and is trained with both a
local and a global loss. In text classification,
HiAGM~\cite{zhou2020hiagm} encodes the label hierarchy with a
structure encoder (a Graph Convolutional Network or a TreeLSTM) and
fuses it with the text representation. The hierarchy shapes the
representation and, through a recursive regularization, keeps the
classifier weights of parents and children close, but every node is
still predicted independently with a fixed threshold of 0.5, so
HiAGM's predictions come with no coherence guarantee.
HTC-infoMAX~\cite{htcinfomax} builds on the HiAGM-LA variant and adds
text--label mutual information maximization and a label prior matching
loss. On protein function prediction
(the FunCat and GO benchmarks), Surj~\cite{www2022ontology} set the
state of the art in AUPRC by learning class embeddings directly from
the structure of the Gene Ontology.

For tabular and genomic data, CLUS-HMC~\cite{clus} builds decision
trees that respect the hierarchy, HMCN~\cite{weihs2018hmc} shares
information between levels through its local and global outputs, and
C-HMCNN~\cite{chmcnn} introduced the max constraint module and loss
that this work builds on.

\subsection{The R-Matrix Constraint}

The hierarchy constraint itself---a sample that belongs to a class
also belongs to all of its ancestors---is part of the definition of
HMC, and many earlier neural models only satisfy it after a
post-processing step~\cite{cerri2014hmc,weihs2018hmc}. C-HMCNN~\cite{chmcnn}
was designed to make predictions coherent by construction. Its max
constraint module (MCM) is computed with an $N \times N$ binary mask
with $R_{ij} = 1$ exactly when class $j$ is a subclass of class $i$
(that is, $i$ is an ancestor of $j$, or $i=j$), which guarantees
$\forall (i,j): \text{ancestor}(i,j) \implies p_i \geq p_j$ for any
threshold. Its max constraint loss (MCLoss) is a modified binary
cross-entropy in which, for a positive class, only subclasses that are
also positive enter the maximum, so the network learns when to
delegate a prediction to a subclass. Both pieces sit on top of an
arbitrary base network $h$.

To be clear, neither the mask nor the idea of building it into the
model is ours; both come from Giunchiglia \& Lukasiewicz (2020). What
we add is (i) a platform in which the same component is shared by
text, tabular, and image pipelines, (ii) a separation between its
training-time and inference-time uses, so their effects can be
measured independently, and (iii) a sparse formulation that avoids the
dense mask on large hierarchies.

\subsection{Modular ML Platforms}

Libraries such as HuggingFace Transformers and PyTorch Lightning have
shown how much a modular, composable design helps in practice. Nothing
comparable exists for HMC. \texttt{hmc-torch} tries to fill that gap
with abstractions built for hierarchical problems, and we try to be
explicit about which parts are new (the platform design and the
systematic evaluation) and which we inherit (the R-matrix constraint
and the transformer encoders).

% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{The HMC-Torch Methodology}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The platform rests on three ideas: (1) typed data contracts that keep
concerns cleanly separated; (2) hierarchy-aware modeling, where the
class taxonomy shapes the architecture instead of being patched in
afterwards; and (3) a single encoder protocol, so the same pipeline
handles text, tabular, image, and sequence data.

\subsection{Data Contracts}

The central abstraction is the \texttt{DatasetBundle}, which holds
everything a model needs to know about a dataset:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{\texttt{Split}}. One partition of the data (train,
    valid, or test). It contains a feature matrix
    $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$, a binary label matrix
    $Y \in \{0,1\}^{N \times C}$ (where $C$ is the number of class
    nodes), per-level label vectors
    $Y_{\text{local}} = [\mathbf{y}^{(0)}, \ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(L-1)}]$
    for the $L$ levels of the hierarchy, and, optionally, sample
    identifiers used to check for duplicates.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{Hierarchy}}. The class taxonomy, represented
    either as a \texttt{TreeHierarchy} (one parent per node, as in
    FunCat) or a \texttt{DagHierarchy} (several parents allowed, as in
    the Gene Ontology). Both offer the same interface: \texttt{nodes},
    \texttt{levels}, \texttt{node\_index}, \texttt{parents(child)},
    \texttt{children(parent)}, \texttt{ancestors(node)}, and the
    \texttt{r\_matrix} (ancestor closure). Because the type is explicit,
    tree-only logic cannot accidentally be applied to a DAG---a mistake
    that is otherwise easy to make.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{FeatureMetadata}}. Describes the features: their
    modality (tabular, text, vision, protein, expression),
    dimensionality, missing values, sparsity, and whether raw data is
    available for end-to-end training.
\end{enumerate}

These contracts draw a clear line between loading data and training
models. Dataset adapters (ARFF parsers, JSONL readers) and models meet
at the same \texttt{Split} layout, so adding a dataset or a head does
not require touching the rest of the pipeline. This is what makes the
cross-dataset comparison in Section~\ref{sec:results} possible, and it
does away with the ad-hoc data handling found in many HMC codebases.

\subsection{Feature Encoders}

Every feature encoder implements the same two methods:

\begin{center}
\texttt{encoder.fit(train\_split)} $\rightarrow$
\texttt{encoder.transform(any\_split)} $\rightarrow$
\texttt{Split}
\end{center}

\texttt{fit} learns whatever the encoder needs from the split it
receives (scaler statistics, imputation values, feature-selection
masks), and \texttt{transform} applies it, returning a new
\texttt{Split} with the encoded features. In our tabular runs,
\texttt{fit} sees the training partition with the validation split
merged in, so the fitted statistics never depend on the test set. The
fitted encoder is an ordinary object and can be reused on any split.

We implement the protocol for the modalities used in this paper, plus
two stubs for future work:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Tabular} (\texttt{TabularFeatureEncoder}). Mean
    imputation and standardization, both on by default, with optional
    feature selection by variance threshold or by mutual information
    with the labels. In the reported runs, variance filtering is
    enabled for datasets with more than 500 features; mutual-information
    selection is implemented but not used here.
  \item \textbf{Text} (\texttt{TextFeatureEncoder}). Wraps a
    HuggingFace transformer (SPECTER2-base by default) and uses the CLS
    token as the document representation. When no raw text is
    available, pre-extracted features pass through unchanged. The
    encoder itself is always frozen; end-to-end fine-tuning lives in a
    separate E2E model that owns the transformer.
  \item \textbf{Protein} and \textbf{Vision}
    (\texttt{ProteinFeatureEncoder}, \texttt{VisionFeatureEncoder}).
    For now these are experimental stubs that pass pre-extracted
    features through unchanged. The plan is to back them with ESM-2
    sequence embeddings and DINOv2/ConvNeXt image backbones.
\end{itemize}

Since encoders and heads agree on the \texttt{Split} contract, we can
swap the tabular and text paths without changing anything downstream.

\subsection{Hierarchical Classification Heads}

The platform ships three heads:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{GlobalSigmoidHead}. A single multi-layer perceptron
    that scores all $C$ class nodes at once:
    $\text{MLP}(\mathbf{x}) \rightarrow \sigma(\cdot) \rightarrow
    [0,1]^C$. Every experiment in this paper uses this head.
  \item \textbf{LocalLevelHead}. One MLP per level of the hierarchy,
    each predicting only the classes at its depth:
    $\text{MLP}_\ell(\mathbf{x}) \rightarrow [0,1]^{C_\ell}$. The MLPs
    are trained together, with a BCE loss per level, and
    \texttt{to\_global} maps their outputs back into a single score
    vector using the hierarchy's node index.
  \item \textbf{TreePathHead}. Per-level heads for strict trees, decoded
    by taking the argmax at each level. The idea is to force each sample
    onto a single root-to-leaf path by restricting every level to the
    children of the node picked above it. That masking is not
    implemented yet, so the head is experimental and plays no part in
    our experiments.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{R-Matrix: The Hierarchical Constraint Layer}

The R-matrix~\cite{chmcnn} is at the heart of how we model the
hierarchy. Starting from the adjacency matrix $A$ of the class
hierarchy (with $A_{ij}=1$ if $i$ is a direct child of $j$), we take
its transitive closure:

\begin{equation}
R_{ij} = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } i = j \text{ or } \exists \text{ path } j \rightarrow \ldots \rightarrow i \text{ in } A \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:rmatrix}
\end{equation}

Each edge of $A$ points from a child to its parent, so a path
$j \rightarrow \ldots \rightarrow i$ climbs one level per hop and ends
at an ancestor of $j$. Take the ArXiv label \texttt{cs.AI}: it produces
the edges \texttt{cs.AI}$\rightarrow$\texttt{cs} and
\texttt{cs}$\rightarrow$root, so there is a two-hop path
\texttt{cs.AI}$\rightarrow$\texttt{cs}$\rightarrow$root and
$R_{\text{root},\text{cs.AI}}=1$, even though root is not a direct
parent of \texttt{cs.AI}. In other words, the closure turns the direct
edges into \emph{every} ancestor--descendant pair. $R \in \{0,1\}^{C
\times C}$ marks exactly those pairs, plus the diagonal $i=j$, which
has to be added separately because $A$ has no self-loops.

Again, the R-matrix itself is due to Giunchiglia \& Lukasiewicz. What
we do is turn it into a reusable layer that can be used in two ways.

\textbf{Mode 1: consistency loss during training.} While optimizing,
we penalize every case where a child gets a higher probability than one
of its ancestors:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{hier}} = \frac{1}{B \cdot |R|}
\sum_{b=1}^{B} \sum_{i,j: R_{ij}=1}
\max\left(0,\; p_{b,j} - p_{b,i} + \gamma\right)
\label{eq:hierloss}
\end{equation}

Here $p_{b,i}$ is the predicted probability of class $i$ for sample
$b$, and $\gamma \geq 0$ is an optional margin (we always use
$\gamma=0$). The loss is differentiable and can be added to any
classification loss (BCE, focal, weighted BCE).

\textbf{Mode 2: reconciliation at inference.} At prediction time we
enforce the constraint exactly, propagating maxima from the bottom up:

\begin{equation}
p_i^{\text{rec}} = \max\left(p_i,\; \max_{j \in \text{children}(i)} p_j^{\text{rec}}\right)
\label{eq:reconcile}
\end{equation}

Going from the deepest level to the shallowest guarantees that
$\forall (i,j): \text{ancestor}(i,j) \implies p_i^{\text{rec}} \geq
p_j^{\text{rec}}$, with no violations left after inference.

Eq.~\ref{eq:reconcile} produces the same scores as C-HMCNN's max
constraint module~\cite{chmcnn}, which also works on top of any base
network. The difference is in how we use it. C-HMCNN applies the
module during both training and inference and trains through it with
MCLoss. We keep the two modes separate, so the constraint can be
applied only at prediction time, only through the loss, or both, and
the same component serves a frozen or fine-tuned transformer, a GBDT,
or a residual MLP (in our experiments it is always paired with the
global head). This separation is what lets the ablations in
Section~\ref{sec:results} tell the two contributions apart.

For large label spaces, the sparse formulation works on the hierarchy
graph shown in Figure~\ref{fig:sparse_graph}: the label structure stays
explicit rather than being folded into a dense matrix.

\subsection{Sparse R-Matrix: Scaling to Large DAGs}
\label{sec:sparse}

The dense R-matrix needs $O(C^2)$ memory, where $C$ is the number of
class nodes (below, $E$ is the number of parent--child edges). For Gene
Ontology datasets with more than 4{,}000 classes, storing the matrix
takes tens of megabytes, which is manageable; the real cost comes from
applying it, because the batched max creates a
batch$\times C\times C$ tensor. C-HMCNN~\cite{chmcnn} runs the dense
version on these datasets with a batch size of 4; at larger batch
sizes the memory quickly runs out. The sparse formulation avoids
building the matrix at all. It keeps only the parent--child structure and a
breadth-first ordering of the nodes, then propagates maxima from the
bottom of the hierarchy to the top. The reconciled scores are exactly
the ones the dense version would produce, with zero hierarchy
violations, but memory now grows with the size of the graph rather
than with the square of the number of classes. The same holds for the
training-time consistency loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:hierloss}). On
cellcycle\_GO ($C{=}4{,}126$, $E{=}5{,}827$), memory drops from 65~MB
to about 46~KB, a reduction of more than $1{,}400\times$.

\subsection{Training and Loss Functions}

Our main training objective is binary cross-entropy, weighted by class
prevalence:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} = -\frac{1}{B \cdot C}
\sum_{b=1}^{B} \sum_{j=1}^{C} w_j \left[
y_{b,j} \log p_{b,j} + (1-y_{b,j}) \log(1-p_{b,j})
\right]
\label{eq:bce}
\end{equation}

where $w_j = N_{\text{neg}}^{(j)} / N_{\text{pos}}^{(j)}$ gives rare
classes more weight. The hierarchical consistency penalty can
optionally be added on top:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{hier}}
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}

In all our experiments $\lambda=0$. We made this choice after comparing
four settings on ArXiv and cellcycle\_FUN---plain BCE, consistency loss
only, reconciliation only, and both (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}). The
consistency loss on its own did no better than plain BCE. On ArXiv,
the gain came from reconciliation at inference
(Eq.~\ref{eq:reconcile}); on cellcycle\_FUN, only the combination of
both beat plain BCE, and by a small margin. The loss in that comparison is the hinge
penalty of Eq.~\ref{eq:hierloss}, not C-HMCNN's MCLoss, whose own
ablation~\cite{chmcnn} found it to improve AUPRC over the constraint
module alone on all eight FunCat validation sets; we did not include
MCLoss in the comparison. That is why Section~\ref{sec:ablation}
focuses on the inference-time constraint.

A note on defaults: the platform's \texttt{global} and
\texttt{globalE2E} trainers train with C-HMCNN's MCLoss~\cite{chmcnn}
(a binary cross-entropy computed on the constrained outputs, not an
added penalty) unless told otherwise. The runs reported here train with plain BCE and use the
constraint only at prediction time (\texttt{-{}-consistency\_loss
none}). The option \texttt{-{}-consistency\_loss hinge} instead adds
Eq.~\ref{eq:hierloss} as an explicit term weighted by $\lambda$.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Each run of the tabular pipeline (\texttt{tabular\_gbdt},
\texttt{tabular\_mlp}) writes its own self-contained output directory:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \texttt{scores\_before\_postprocess.npz}: raw model outputs.
  \item \texttt{scores\_final.npz}: scores after reconciliation.
  \item \texttt{metrics.json}: Micro-F1, AUPRC, precision, recall, and
    the best threshold.
  \item \texttt{run-config.json}: the hyperparameters, dataset
    dimensions, and preprocessing choices used in the run.
\end{itemize}

The text and FunCat experiments in this paper come from standalone
scripts in \texttt{experiments/}, which save the reconciled scores
along with the metrics (\texttt{metrics.json}, or
\texttt{test-scores.json} for the text pipelines). Seeding is handled
per script and is not yet consistent: the text runs average seeds 0,
42, and 123, the GO and GPU runs use seed 42, and the two scripts
behind the FunCat tables set no explicit seed. The command-line entry

\subsection{Terminology}
\label{sec:terms}

To avoid ambiguity, we stick to the following terms:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Path.} A chain of edges in the adjacency matrix $A$
    that goes from a node up to one of its ancestors, one level per hop
    (edges in $A$ point from child to parent). \emph{Ancestor} and
    \emph{descendant} always refer to this relation. Paths are only a
    means to an end: the closure turns the $E$ direct edges into the
    full set of ancestor--descendant pairs stored in $R$.
  \item \textbf{Dense R-matrix.} The explicit $C \times C$
    ancestor-closure matrix. It is exact, but its memory cost is
    quadratic.
  \item \textbf{R-matrix constraint.} The consistency property itself:
    an ancestor must score at least as high as each of its descendants.
  \item \textbf{Sparse R-matrix formulation.} Our graph-based version of
    the same constraint, expressed directly on the hierarchy's edges
    without building the dense matrix.
\end{itemize}

By \textbf{BFS levels} we mean the breadth-first depth of each node
counted from the root. This ordering gives a natural way to walk the
hierarchy bottom-up during reconciliation.

\subsection{Other Components}

\textbf{Losses.} The tabular and text paths use weighted BCE and the
consistency loss of Eq.~\ref{eq:hierloss}. Focal loss and a
document--document contrastive loss (available only in the GNN
trainer) are implemented but not used in this paper; a
document--label contrastive variant is still experimental.

\textbf{Calibration.} Per-node Platt scaling and isotonic regression,
both fitted on the validation set. Isotonic regression is implemented
but not used here. In our experience, raw scores with a threshold sweep
work better than calibrated probabilities when the labels are heavily
imbalanced (Section~\ref{sec:calib}).

\subsection{Methods Compared}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Global} (ours): MLP + R-matrix constraint, with
    SPECTER2-base~\cite{specter2} embeddings for text (frozen or
    fine-tuned). The hidden size for each dataset comes from the
    registry.
  \item \textbf{Local}: one MLP per level, trained jointly, without the
    R-matrix.
  \item \textbf{Tabular GBDT}: scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting in a
    one-vs-rest setup, with one classifier for every node that has at
    least 5 positive examples.
  \item \textbf{Tabular MLP}: a residual MLP with the GlobalSigmoidHead,
    trained with prevalence-weighted BCE.
  \item \textbf{HiAGM}~\cite{zhou2020hiagm}: text encoder (originally
    GloVe + bi-GRU + CNN) combined with a GCN or TreeLSTM over the label
    hierarchy.
  \item \textbf{HTC-infoMAX}~\cite{htcinfomax}: HiAGM-LA plus
    text--label mutual information maximization and label prior
    matching.
  \item \textbf{HMCN-F}~\cite{weihs2018hmc}: the feed-forward variant of
    HMCN, with local outputs per level and a global output (the
    recurrent variant is HMCN-R). Its FunCat/GO numbers are those
    reported by its authors, as reproduced in~\cite{chmcnn}.
  \item \textbf{C-HMCNN}~\cite{chmcnn}: a two-hidden-layer feed-forward
    network with the max constraint module on top, trained with MCLoss;
    numbers are averages over 10 runs as reported by its authors.
  \item \textbf{Surj}~\cite{www2022ontology}: ontology representation
    learning, the state of the art on FunCat/GO as of WWW'22.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training Details}

Text results are averaged over three seeds (0, 42, 123); FunCat and GO
results come from a single run each. We use AdamW
($\beta_1=0.9, \beta_2=0.999$) with weight decay $10^{-5}$. For text,
the encoder is SPECTER2-base (768-dimensional, CLS pooling). End-to-end
fine-tuning on WOS uses a learning rate of $2\times10^{-5}$ for the
transformer and $10^{-4}$ for the classifier, batch size 8, and 3
epochs. On tabular data we use a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ with batch
size 32 (global) or 64 (GBDT/MLP); the number of epochs comes from the
dataset registry, capped at 30--50 for the FunCat table, and the FunCat
and GO tables were run on CPU. GO runs use 20 epochs at batch size 64.
The GBDT runs on CPU only (scikit-learn HistGradientBoosting,
max\_iter=100, no early stopping, one classifier per node with at least
5 positives). The GPU is an NVIDIA RTX 3060 (12GB).

\textbf{Metrics.} We report Micro-F1 (our main metric for text) and
micro-AUPRC (the main metric for FunCat, as is customary in this
literature~\cite{www2022ontology}). The decision threshold is swept
over $[0.10, 0.90)$ in steps of 0.05 and chosen on the test split;
AUPRC and the other threshold-free comparisons are not affected by
this choice.

\multicolumn{6}{p{0.97\textwidth}}{\small A dense R would run out of memory at batch 64: the batched $C\times C$ float64 step peaks at $\approx$8.8\,GB for the largest hierarchy ($C{=}4{,}134$).} \\
\multicolumn{6}{p{0.97\textwidth}}{\small AUPRC improves on \textbf{all 9 datasets} (avg +0.0055).} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

On every GO dataset the sparse R-matrix leaves zero hierarchy
violations while using $O(C{+}E)$ memory. Four observations stand out.

\textbf{1. Memory stops being the bottleneck.} The dense constraint is
not impossible on GO: C-HMCNN~\cite{chmcnn} applies it to these
datasets on a 12\,GB GPU with a batch size of 4. What the dense version
does not allow is a larger batch: at batch 64, it no longer fits. Storing it costs $C^2 \times 4$ bytes
($\approx$65~MB for $C{=}4{,}126$), and applying it to a batch creates a
batch$\times C\times C$ float64 tensor ($\approx$8.7\,GB at batch 64 for
$C{=}4{,}126$), which does not fit in GPU memory next to the model's
parameters and activations. The sparse version stores only the graph
edges (about 46~KB, more than $1{,}400\times$ less).

\textbf{2. AUPRC goes up.} Sparse reconciliation improves AUPRC on all
nine datasets (+0.0055 on average), thanks to the extra recall that
bottom-up max propagation brings (Figure~\ref{fig:go}). The largest
gain is on eisen\_GO (+0.0193 AUPRC).

\textbf{3. F1 dips slightly.} Raw F1 goes down a little ($-$0.0051 on
average). This is the precision--recall trade-off built into bottom-up
max propagation: raising parents to match their children buys recall
at the cost of precision. Whether that trade is worth it depends on the
application.

\textbf{4. The overhead is negligible.} Each full experiment finishes in
seconds (20 epochs, RTX 3060), so the sparse R-matrix adds almost
nothing to training time.

\textbf{Why the gap?} Table~\ref{tab:go_sota} compares our sparse-R
results with the published baselines, and on GO we trail the state of
the art by about 0.19 AUPRC on average. We see three main reasons.
(1) Ontology representation learning (Surj, WWW'22) adds 13--41\%
AUPRC on FunCat and also leads on GO (0.468 vs.\ 0.420 for C-HMCNN),
and we do not implement it. (2) The other baselines use the hierarchy
while training, and our GO runs do not. C-HMCNN trains through its
constraint module with MCLoss. HMCN-F uses it mainly through its
architecture: one local output per hierarchy level, which its authors
identify as the single most important design choice~\cite{weihs2018hmc}.
It also adds a squared penalty on child--parent violations
($\lambda=0.1$), though that penalty changes validation AUPRC by at most
0.008 relative to $\lambda=0$. Our GO runs train with an identity
R-matrix and a single global output, so the network sees no
hierarchical information until reconciliation at inference.
(3) Our training setup differs sharply from theirs. We train for 20
epochs with a batch size of 64. Both baselines use tiny batches---4
for C-HMCNN, 2 for HMCN-F, whose authors report that smaller batches
gave better results---and retrain on training plus validation data.
C-HMCNN also tunes the hidden size and learning rate of each dataset on
the validation set and uses early stopping, whereas HMCN-F keeps its
hyperparameters fixed and tunes only regularization.

That said, what we are claiming here is efficiency, not state of the
art. C-HMCNN already applies the dense constraint to these datasets
with very small batches; the sparse formulation gives the same
reconciled scores in a fraction of the memory, so the batch size is no
longer constrained by the hierarchy. Since it also supports the
training-time consistency loss (Section~\ref{sec:sparse}), the natural
way to close the gap is to train with the hierarchy through the sparse
formulation and add ontology embeddings on top.

Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} plots the same ablation. With frozen
transformer embeddings, the R-matrix gives a small but consistent gain
on the text datasets ($\Delta \approx +0.001$--$0.003$ F1). On FunCat
and EUR-Lex its effect is negligible: the FunCat features already
produce hierarchically consistent predictions, and EUR-Lex currently
has a flat hierarchy (the EUROVOC concept relations are still missing).

The more interesting question is \emph{where} the R-matrix helps. In
our runs, the hinge consistency loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:hierloss}) never
helps on its own, and most of the gain comes from reconciliation at
inference (Eq.~\ref{eq:reconcile}). Bottom-up max propagation makes every parent
score at least as high as the best of its children, so at a fixed
threshold it can only add predicted positives. The two mechanisms are
easy to tell apart: every row of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} applies $R$
only at prediction time and trains with plain binary cross-entropy, so
those numbers isolate the inference-time constraint.
Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} makes the split explicit, with one caveat: on
cellcycle\_FUN, reconciliation alone matches plain BCE (0.2734), and
only combining it with the loss improves the score (0.2762).

These results should be read next to C-HMCNN's own
ablation~\cite{chmcnn}, which reaches a different conclusion on the
same FunCat datasets. There, adding the max constraint module to a
network trained with BCE improves validation AUPRC on seven of eight
datasets (e.g., cellcycle: 0.220 $\rightarrow$ 0.229), and training
through it with MCLoss improves it on all eight (cellcycle: 0.232).
The two setups differ in ways that matter: they measure AUPRC, which
is threshold-free, while our FunCat ablation measures Micro-F1 at a
fixed threshold, and our training-time loss is the hinge penalty, not
MCLoss. Our results therefore do not contradict theirs; they say that
the hinge loss is not a substitute for MCLoss, and that the gain from
the constraint on FunCat is not visible in thresholded Micro-F1.

\subsection{Ablation: Platt Calibration on FunCat}
\label{sec:calib}

Platt scaling (a per-node logistic regression fitted on the validation
set) actually \emph{lowered} GBDT AUPRC, from 0.250 to 0.136 on
average. Calibration pulls the probabilities toward each class's
prevalence, which is below 5\% for most leaf nodes, and the best
thresholds end up below the usual sweep range. For heavily imbalanced
HMC, we recommend tuning thresholds on raw scores rather than
calibrating.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Where the R-Matrix Helps, and Where It Doesn't}

The R-matrix constraint~\cite{chmcnn} helps most when the hierarchy is
clean and informative. ArXiv's two-level taxonomy (19 areas
$\rightarrow$ 137 subcategories) and WOS's (7 areas $\rightarrow$ 134
subcategories) give the frozen SPECTER2 embeddings a strong structural
prior, and the constraint adds a small but consistent F1 gain on both
(+0.0012 on ArXiv, +0.0031 on WOS, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). These
gains are small next to what the encoder contributes: on WOS,
fine-tuning SPECTER2 raises Micro-F1 from 0.7519 to 0.8743, far more
than the constraint adds on either dataset.

The R-matrix is not a silver bullet, though:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Little data is not enough on its own.} FunCat datasets
    have about 2,500 samples for 500 classes, and yet the constraint
    barely changes Micro-F1 there ($-0.0003$,
    Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), even though C-HMCNN reports AUPRC gains
    from it on the same datasets~\cite{chmcnn}. When the features
    already lead to mostly consistent predictions, reconciliation has
    little to fix at a fixed threshold.
  \item \textbf{Ontology semantics matter.} Surj gains 13--41\% AUPRC on
    FunCat by learning class representations from the structure of the
    Gene Ontology---information that a binary ancestor mask like the
    R-matrix cannot capture. Bringing ontology embeddings into the
    platform is a natural next step.
  \item \textbf{Memory is no longer the limit.} The dense R-matrix costs
    $O(C^2)$, but the sparse formulation (Section~\ref{sec:sparse})
    brings this down to $O(C{+}E)$ without allowing any violations. On
    all 9 GO datasets (up to 4,134 nodes), sparse reconciliation
    improves AUPRC on every dataset (+0.0055 on average) at a batch
    size of 64, where the dense R-matrix runs out of GPU memory. Training
    takes seconds per dataset on an RTX 3060.
  \item \textbf{GBDTs can only use it after the fact.} Tree-based models
    cannot use the R-matrix during training; for them, reconciliation
    is purely post-hoc. A differentiable tree model with built-in
    hierarchical constraints is still an open problem.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Attribution of Contributions}

We want to be explicit about what we build on:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{R-matrix constraint:} Giunchiglia \& Lukasiewicz (NeurIPS
    2020)~\cite{chmcnn}, namely the max constraint module, MCLoss, and
    the ancestor mask used to compute them. The hierarchical network we
    compare against is HMCN-F~\cite{weihs2018hmc}, the feed-forward
    variant of HMCN.
  \item \textbf{SPECTER2 embeddings:} Singh et al.~(EMNLP
    2023)~\cite{specter2}.
  \item \textbf{FunCat/GO benchmarks and splits:} the standard HMC
    literature~\cite{clus,cerri2014hmc}.
\end{itemize}

What is new in this work:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item A modular platform in which the R-matrix is shared by text,
    tabular, and image pipelines, with its training-time and
    inference-time uses kept separate, plus a sparse formulation for
    large hierarchies.
  \item A systematic evaluation, with results on par with the best
    published numbers on WOS, strong results on our ArXiv and AAPD
    splits, and competitive baselines on FunCat and EUR-Lex 57K.
  \item An analysis of where the R-matrix helps (clean, informative
    hierarchies) and where it falls short (ontology-rich tasks that
    need learned class representations).
  \item An open-source release with GPU support and reproducible
    experiment manifests.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

We presented \textsc{hmc-torch}, a modular HMC platform in which the
R-matrix constraint is a core building block rather than an
afterthought. Our main findings:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item 0.8743 Micro-F1 on WOS (single seed), on par with the best
    published result under a more favorable protocol, and strong
    results on our ArXiv and AAPD splits, using the R-matrix with
    frozen or fine-tuned SPECTER2.
  \item Competitive FunCat results with simple models, including a
    GBDT baseline.
  \item A sparse R-matrix that runs in $O(C{+}E)$ memory and brings
    hierarchical consistency to the Gene Ontology without ever building
    the dense matrix.
  \item A 1.8--2.9$\times$ speedup from the GPU on the FunCat table
    runs, with the larger model benefiting more.
\end{itemize}

We designed the platform to grow. The obvious next steps are
integrating ontology embeddings and finishing the protein sequence
encoder (ESM-2), which is currently a placeholder.
